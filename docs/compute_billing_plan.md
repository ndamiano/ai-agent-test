# Compute & Billing Plan

Maestro functions by running inference against a small LLM, small image models, small 3d models and small audio models. This design enables us to utilize rented consumer hardware to actually generate the game, at much cheaper rates than frontier models. A current game generated by Maestro costs less than 20 minutes of compute time, but the expectation is that as time goes on and we make improvements and generate larger, more in depth games that number will rise.

While the main expected income is from generating games, we also have the ability to potentially sell games that people have generated, enabling them to get more credits and us to bring in more income. This plan is TBD, but the idea is to sell a game for $0.99 and (for instance) every 10 copies sold gives the creator a credit (or potentially they get 20% of all sales; TBD) is a flywheel to get people into our ecosystem and make us more money. 

---

## 1. The product & price

- A credit costs $5.
- Spending a credit on a game starts the build and grants that run 14,400 seconds of 5090 GPU-execution.
- When a run's compute is exhausted, the user can spend another credit to extend the same run by
  another 4 hours.
- The frontend shows an obfuscated bar that drains as compute is consumed — no numbers, no
  seconds. The bar is `remaining / granted`.

The 4 hours is a *ceiling*, not a target. Most games should finish well under it; the ceiling exists
so a thrashing build can't burn unbounded compute on our dime.

---

## 2. Unit economics

Cost invariant is 5090 GPU-seconds.

|Item|Cost|Margin|
|---|---|---|
| Credit | $5.00 |-|
| RunPod 5090 hour | $0.99 |-|
| 4 GPU-hr fully burned |$3.96|$1.04|
| 40 GPU-min build | $0.66 |$4.34 margin|

$1 is the floor, hit only if a run consumes its entire 4-hour ceiling. Faster builds consume less GPU and widen the margin. This aligns the incentive perfectly: every quality improvement that lets a game finish in less compute both improves UX and fattens the margin.

Parallelization is a UX optimization only. GPU-hours is the cost invariant, so 10 GPUs for 4 min each costs the same as 1 GPU for 40 min. It shortens wall-clock (nice for asset fan-out) but does not change the bill.

It is also working into the cost of purchasing hardware to keep a warmed pod at much lower cost. Idle cost of an owned 5090 is negligible, and runtime cost of an owned 5090 is closer to $0.20 per hour, leading to a better experience (always warm queues) and higher profits. The cost for systems that can handle this is upfont prohibitively expensive at >5,000 per unit, meaning ~9 months for break even at 100% utilization. When profit is more steady, this should be re-investigated.

### Utilization

We bill users for GPU-execution-seconds but pay RunPod for wall-clock pod uptime; every second a pod exists, including cold start, queue idle, and gaps between requests costs money. Given that, our goal is to maximize utilization.

Every second of uptime that isn't used for inference is pure loss, and therefore we should autoscale aggressively. This is one of the core metrics that we should be watching. If utilization drops below 90%, we should be looking into why and fixing it.

## 3. Metering

We will be metering on GPU inference time. Each request will return the time it took to execute, which will be subtracted from the alloted budget for the game. Utilizing this, we can handle multiple requests at once, subtracting each on completion, allowing for a better user experience.

Additionally, we can utilize different GPUs with multipliers on seconds. For example, an H100 rental is $2.89 per GPU hour, which we can subtract 3 seconds per GPU second, but allows us to process either faster or utilizing higher quality models.

This does come with downsides, as we subtract from the budget on completion, this leads to potentially overdrawing, which will eat into our profit margins. If we find this to be a problem, we will need to estimate request costs and enqueue smaller amounts to limit overdraws.

---

## 4. Queue architecture

Given the fact that downtime is strictly loss-y, it is likely to be beneficial to utilize a separate queue and unit per model and endpoint. Time spent switching models and switching inference types (comfyUI vs llama-cpp) is purely loss. We will need to carefully evaluate the models we utilize to ensure their licensing is viable, but in general, we will use the following methods and models for V1:

### Large Language Models
Llama.cpp will be our primary inference provider, and we will utilize the `QWEN` family of models. Specifically, we are targeting Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-NVFP4.

### Image model (diffusion)
We will be using ComfyUI as our primary interface for generating images, and we'll be utilizing the illustrious family of models. Specifically we are targeting waiIllustriousSDXL_v170.

### Topography (3d models) model
We will be utilizing the trellis family of models.

## 5. Failure modes

There are two main failure modes, infrastructure failures and quality failures as well as a functional failure mode of churn.

Infrastructure failures lead to bad user experience, when their build stalls, but ultimately it doesn't affect their generation. Our requests are all retryable, and by not charging until a result has been returned, the user will not see a failure as a cost to themself.

Quality failures are when what comes out isn't a game or is low quality. This is broken logic, failed image / 3d model generation, etc. This case will naturally diminish over time, and we should add language similar to "Game generation is non deterministic. We try our best to generate good games, but the game you get out may not be a game you like. If the game is functional, outside of extreme cases, we will not refund you."

Churn failures are when the model gets stuck in a loop. It tries and tries and tries to get a result out, but for whatever reason cannot. The reason may be that it added some bad code, it might be that it cannot figure out how to progress, but no matter what the reason, it's not making forward progress. We have stall detection in place, with the goal of not wasting compute, but we may need to build a better UX to help people understand WHY it's stalling, and enable them to fix it, or provide a process for an internal developer to fix it.

## 6. Open decisions

- **exec-seconds source**: does the llama.cpp router surface per-request execution time, or does the
  worker wrapper measure it? (Responses `usage` gives tokens, not seconds.)
- **Pod packing**: confirm one-model-per-pod for all three modalities, or co-locate small ones.
- **Weights delivery**: network volume vs baked image (cold-start latency vs pull size vs storage
  cost).
- **Spot vs on-demand** per queue (preemption tolerance vs price).
- **Idle-timeout per queue** — the cold-start-UX vs idle-cost tradeoff, tuned per modality.
