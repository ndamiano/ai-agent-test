# Finance Information

GameSummoner functions by running inference against open weight LLMs, image, and 3d models. This
design enables us to utilize rented consumer hardware to actually generate the game, at much cheaper
rates than frontier models. A current game generated by GameSummoner costs less than 20 minutes of
compute time, but the expectation is that as time goes on and we make improvements and generate
larger, more in depth games that number will rise.

Currently, the only monetization in place is selling credits, however, in the future, a store could
be built to sell games that our users created.

## The product & price

- A credit costs $5.
- Spending a credit on a game starts the build and grants that run $3.00 of GPU-execution.
- When a run's compute is exhausted, the user can spend another credit to extend the same run by
  another $3.00.
- The frontend shows an obfuscated bar that drains as compute is consumed. The bar is
  `remaining / granted`.

The $3.00 is a ceiling to ensure profitability, but will ideally not be reached.

## Unit economics

Our unit cost is simply GPU time used per game. There is some complication in parallelization, in
that our cost is lower than implied by the following, but ultimately it boils down to:

|Item|Cost|Margin|
|---|---|---|
| Credit | $5.00 |-|
| Stripe | $0.45 |-|
| RunPod RTX 6000 hour | $2.19 |-|
| Grant fully burned |$3.00|$1.55|
| Mean game cost | $1.81 |$2.74|

$1.55 is the floor, hit only if a run consumes its entire grant. Faster builds consume less GPU and
widen the margin. This aligns the incentive perfectly: every quality improvement that lets a game
finish in less compute both improves UX and fattens the margin.

Parallelization is a UX optimization only. GPU-hours is the cost invariant, so 10 GPUs for 4 min
each costs the same as 1 GPU for 40 min. It shortens wall-clock (nice for asset fan-out) but does
not change the bill.

### Utilization

We bill users for GPU-execution-seconds but pay RunPod for wall-clock pod uptime; every second a pod
exists, including cold start, queue idle, and gaps between requests costs money. Given that, our
goal is to maximize utilization.

Every second of uptime that isn't used for inference is pure loss, and therefore we should autoscale
aggressively. This is one of the core metrics that we should be watching. Utilization is unmeasured
before we go to prod.

## Metering

When we request a GPU, we record the cost per hour for that specific device (which can vary due to
circumstances such as GPU type or whether it's spot.) When a job completes, we calculate billed time
based on the job's reported time, and the price of the GPU, which is deducted from the game's
compute budget.

### Overdraw

Because we only deduct on completion, this can lead to a build overdrawing. While in theory a build
is limited by the compute, we do not stop in flight requests due to budget. Instead, when a request
is queued, we estimate the total cost of that build's requests in the queue and if this request's
estimate would bring us over the limit, we reject it. This does not stop overdraw, but it bounds it
to one request per queue.

## Queue architecture

Downtime is strictly loss, and so time spent switching models on a single card would be money spent
not doing work. In order to mitigate this downtime as much as possible, we give each queue its own
set of pods, aggressively autoscaled down, to keep downtime on the pod itself to a minimum.

While it would be ideal to have the pods die as soon as they hit an empty queue, the startup time
combined with the fact that the queue doesn't necessarily immediately rehydrate after a request, as
there are sometimes slow running python programs or build gates, requires us to give the pods a
keep alive time.

## Failure modes

The two main failure modes are infrastructure failures and quality failures.

Infrastructure failures lead to bad user experience and monetary waste for GameSummoner, but do not
lead to build failures or incomplete games for users. When an infrastructure failure causes a build
to drop a request, we do not debit the user's game, so while a user's experience may be hampered,
the product is not ultimately negatively affected.

Quality failures are when what comes out is low quality for one reason or another. This might be
because the model requested bad images, or it might be because the logic is broken for solving a
puzzle. This case will diminish over time, and while it isn't ideal, as long as a game is functional
we do not offer refunds.

We additionally have a minor failure mode of build churn. This is when a build stalls and bounces
back and forth between reads or other stalling actions. This is partially mitigated with a turn cap
of 200 on the build, but will be a continual fight as our service evolves.
