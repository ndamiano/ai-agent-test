# Billing Plan

GameSummoner functions by running inference against open weight LLMs, image, and 3d models. This
design enables us to utilize rented consumer hardware to actually generate the game, at much cheaper
rates than frontier models. A current game generated by GameSummoner costs less than 20 minutes of
compute time, but the expectation is that as time goes on and we make improvements and generate
larger, more in depth games that number will rise.

While the main expected income is from generating games, another lever exists in the form of selling
games people have created with GameSummoner.

## The product & price

- A credit costs $5.
- Spending a credit on a game starts the build and grants that run $3.00 of GPU-execution in micros.
- When a run's compute is exhausted, the user can spend another credit to extend the same run by
  another $3.00.
- The frontend shows an obfuscated bar that drains as compute is consumed. The bar is
  `remaining / granted`.

The $3.00 is a ceiling to ensure profitability, but will ideally not be reached.

## Unit economics

Our unit cost is simply GPU time used per game. There is some complication in paralelization, in
that our cost is lower than implied by the following, but ultimately it boils down to:

|Item|Cost|Margin|
|---|---|---|
| Credit | $5.00 |-|
| Grant fully burned |$3.00|$2.00|
| RunPod RTX 6000 hour | $2.21 |-|
| 40 GPU-min build | $1.48 |$3.52 margin|

$2 is the floor, hit only if a run consumes its entire grant. Faster builds consume less GPU and
widen the margin. This aligns the incentive perfectly: every quality improvement that lets a game
finish in less compute both improves UX and fattens the margin.

Parallelization is a UX optimization only. GPU-hours is the cost invariant, so 10 GPUs for 4 min
each costs the same as 1 GPU for 40 min. It shortens wall-clock (nice for asset fan-out) but does
not change the bill.

### Utilization

We bill users for GPU-execution-seconds but pay RunPod for wall-clock pod uptime; every second a pod
exists, including cold start, queue idle, and gaps between requests costs money. Given that, our
goal is to maximize utilization.

Every second of uptime that isn't used for inference is pure loss, and therefore we should autoscale
aggressively. This is one of the core metrics that we should be watching. If utilization drops below
90%, we should be looking into why and fixing it.

## Metering

When we request a GPU, we record the cost per hour for that specific device (which can vary due to
circumstances such as GPU type or whether it's spot.) When a job completes, we calculate billed time
based on the job's reported time, and the price of the GPU, which is deducted from the game's
compute budget.

### Overdraw

Because we only deduct on completion, this can lead to a build overdrawing. While in theory a build
is limited by the compute, we do not stop in flight requests due to budget. Instead, when a request
is queued, we estimate the total cost of that build's requests in the queue and if this request's
estimate would bring us over the limit, we reject it. This does not stop overdraw, but it mitigates
it.

## Queue architecture

Downtime is strictly loss, and so time spent switching models on a single card would be money spent
not doing work. In order to mitigate this downtime as much as possible, we give each queue its own
set of pods, aggressively autoscaled down, to keep downtime on the pod itself to a minimum.

While it would be ideal to have the pods die as soon as they hit an empty queue, the startup time
combined with the fact that the queue doesn't necessarily immediately rehydrate after a request, as
there are some times slow running python programs or build gates, requires us to give the pods a
keep alive time.

## 5. Failure modes

There are two main failure modes, infrastructure failures and quality failures as well as a functional failure mode of churn.

Infrastructure failures lead to bad user experience, when their build stalls, but ultimately it doesn't affect their generation. Our requests are all retryable, and the worker measures `exec_seconds` itself (`worker/agent.py`) and reports it on completion — the inference server is never asked — so a job that never returns debits nothing.

Quality failures are when what comes out isn't a game or is low quality. This is broken logic, failed image / 3d model generation, etc. This case will naturally diminish over time, and we should add language similar to "Game generation is non deterministic. We try our best to generate good games, but the game you get out may not be a game you like. If the game is functional, outside of extreme cases, we will not refund you."

Churn failures are when the model gets stuck in a loop. It tries and tries and tries to get a result out, but for whatever reason cannot. The reason may be that it added some bad code, it might be that it cannot figure out how to progress, but no matter what the reason, it's not making forward progress.

**There is no stall detection.** Three things bound a churning build, none of which notice churn as such: the step cap (200 turns), the compute budget (a refused enqueue ends the run), and the repeated-identical-call counter — a failing tool call resent with identical arguments is counted per call and the count told back to the model, leaving what to do about it as the model's decision. Detecting churn *as churn* would mean a rule the model can't satisfy, which is the shape `CLAUDE.md` forbids. What is missing is the UX half: nothing tells the human WHY a build is spinning, or lets them intervene before the cap. That gap is real and unowned.
