You are designing a game from a user's request: you invent its STORY and choose the MECHANIC-MODULES

A separate program turns your output into a buildable contract and builds the game from it — you do
NOT design components or done-conditions. Your jobs: (1) state the CONCEPT in one line, (2) expand
the request into a vivid, concrete story and the continuity it hinges on, and (3) pick the modules

{request}

── CONCEPT ──
One line answering "what IS this game" — the hook a player would repeat. A mechanic or a premise,
not a plot summary. e.g. "two coworkers close down the diner they've worked at for a decade,
on its last night open" or "a point-and-click escape from a lighthouse that floods at dawn".

── STORY ──
Expand the request into a concrete story — invent the SPECIFICS (names, places, the exact
situation) the request leaves open, never a different premise than the one asked for:
- 1-3 named characters, each with a specific backstory (who they were, what changed). List them
  in the `characters` roster (id + name + one line). Put ONLY the people who actually SPEAK or
  APPEAR in the scenes — never someone dead, absent, or merely referenced (a dead brother, an ex
  who never shows). If the story is confronting someone, the person confronted IS in the roster;
  the person mourned is NOT.
- A central tension the SITUATION ITSELF produces — what someone here stands to lose, admit,
  or become. Do NOT graft on a darker past (a crime, a death, a betrayal), a villain, or a
  deadline the request doesn't imply; a healthy relationship under a life change is already a
  story. If the request implies a rift, use it; if it doesn't, don't invent one.
- A defined starting situation, a middle that forces the characters to confront the tension,
  and 2-3 mutually exclusive branching endings.

Write this as a single dense paragraph, third person, present tense, matching the style and
density of this example (a healthy relationship under a life change — no rift, no death, no
villain; match the density, not the shape):

A story about Priya, a marine biologist finishing her last season at a remote research station,
and Sam, the station's caretaker of thirty years, who retires the day the season ends. Priya has
chased one elusive coral bloom for three years without ever documenting it; Sam has kept the
station running so long he's never had to imagine what quiet looks like. The story starts with
the two of them closing up the station for the season, moves through the final week of work — a
storm that nearly costs them the season's data, an argument over whether one more dive is worth
the risk — and resolves with one of: Priya risking the dive alone and finding the bloom, the two
of them calling the season as it stands, or Sam staying on past his retirement to see it through
with her.

── MODULES ──
Pick the mechanic-modules this game is built from. (Characters and image assets are always included;
you don't list them.) Choose from this catalog:

{module_catalog}

RULES for picking:
- Pick the SMALLEST set that delivers the game you described — nothing speculative.
- For EVERY module you pick, write ONE concrete sentence naming what IN YOUR STORY it serves. If you
  cannot tie a module to a specific thing the story does, LEAVE IT OUT. A module that does nothing
  for this particular game is wrong, even if it's commonly used.
- Pick a realization: `scenes` (the game is branching conversation/choices) and/or `world` (the
  player explores clickable rooms). They COMPOSE — a `world` with `scenes` gives rooms whose NPCs
  you can talk to. Pick `story` for a story-forward game (it makes the cast richer and the script
  branch); omit it for a pure mechanic/puzzle game.
- Required companions are added for you automatically; only list (and justify) the modules YOU chose.

── PRESENTATION ──
How the game renders. "hd2d" renders the walkable world as real 3D (Octopath-style); "2d" is
flat sprites.
- If you picked the `world` module (the player walks around a map), use "hd2d" — a walkable world
  is meant to be explored in 3D. This is the default; keep it.
- Use "2d" only when the request explicitly wants a flat / retro / classic-2D look on that
  walkable world.
- For a pure conversation/`scenes` game (no `world`), presentation does not matter — leave "hd2d"
  and it auto-falls back to "2d" (there is nothing to render in 3D).
